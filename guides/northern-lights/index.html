Where to see the northern lights near Anchorage

Spots, forecasts and cloudy-night plans

Guides · Northern lights

Yes — you can see the northern lights from the Anchorage area, but usually not from a lit downtown street. Get away from the city glow, look north, and give yourself a clear, dark sky. Sightings are never guaranteed, so plan for more than one night, and always check the live forecast before you head out.

This guide is for people already in Anchorage or Southcentral, not a trip to Fairbanks. Fairbanks sits closer to the auroral oval, so the odds there are often better. From here, you still have a real shot on a strong, clear night, especially from a dark lot with an open view north. If the lights don't come out, there is still plenty on the weekend list, plus Christmas and holiday events and things to do in December.

How to read the forecast

Two official forecasts matter, and they answer different questions:

  • UAF Geophysical Institute aurora forecast — a multi-day look at how active the lights may be, and where the auroral oval is sitting relative to Alaska.
  • NOAA 30-minute aurora forecast — a shorter-range oval map, useful in the hours before you go out.

A strong aurora forecast is only half the story in Southcentral. Clouds wash the show out just as often as a quiet sun does. Check the National Weather Service Anchorage forecast for cloud cover, and look again right before you leave — both the oval and the clouds can change fast.

If you see a Kp or activity number, treat higher values as “the oval may dip farther south,” which helps Anchorage more than it helps Fairbanks. A modest night can still put on a show if the sky is dark and clear. A strong night under a solid overcast will not.

Best months and hours

In Anchorage the sky is dark enough from about mid-August through mid-April. The most reliable stretch is September through March, when nights are long. Around the September and March equinoxes, geomagnetic activity often picks up. December and January give you the most hours of darkness, even if you have to dress for it.

The lights can appear any time after dark, but the best window is often about 10 PM–3 AM Alaska time, with many of the stronger displays clustering near midnight. A bright moon will wash out a faint aurora; a new moon is easier on the eyes, though a moonlit snowscape can still be beautiful if the lights are strong.

Summer is too bright. If you're here in June or July, a planetarium show is a better bet than a hillside late at night.

Where to go near Anchorage

Drive times below are typical from downtown Anchorage in clear conditions. Add time for ice, snow or weekend traffic. Park only in real lots — never on a highway shoulder.

  • Point WoronzofWest Anchorage, near the airport · About 15 minutes (6 miles)A coastal park at the northwest edge of town, with an open view north across Cook Inlet. It's the closest real option if you don't want a long drive. It can be very windy, and jets from the airport pass close by. Check posted park hours, and dress warmer than you think.
  • Glen AlpsAnchorage Hillside, Chugach State Park · About 25–30 minutes (15 miles)The classic in-town viewpoint. The lot sits high above the bowl, with a short walk to an overlook facing north over the city. The hillside road and the lot can be icy. Alaska State Parks charges a $5 daily parking fee; if you stay past midnight, that can mean a second daily fee. Toilets are at the trailhead. See the Glen Alps trailhead page for directions.
  • Eagle River Nature CenterEnd of Eagle River Road, Chugach State Park · About 40 minutes (26 miles)A quieter valley that feels farther away than it is. Trails stay open around the clock; the building itself is usually open Friday–Sunday, 10 AM–5 PM, from October through April. Parking is $5 per vehicle for 24 hours, paid at the station by the door (state park annual passes are not accepted here). See ernc.org for current trail notes. Do not park along Eagle River Road where signs say not to. Cell service can be spotty.
  • Eklutna Tailrace and the Knik River ValleyNorth on the Glenn Highway · About 45 minutes (34 miles)Darker skies and a wide northern horizon, without mountains blocking a low aurora. This is often the better bet when the lights are sitting low, or when Anchorage itself is hazy with city glow. It's rural, dark and cold — bring a light and winter gear, and use a proper lot, not the highway shoulder. Eklutna Lake is a little farther up the same drainage (about an hour from downtown) if you want the lake trailhead; that lot also has a $5 state park parking fee.
  • GirdwoodAbout 1 hour south (42 miles)The mountains block a lot of Anchorage's glow, so the town itself is darker. The tradeoff is that those same mountains can hide a low display to the north. Alyeska Resort offers a northern lights wake-up call to hotel guests. A reasonable add-on if you're already staying in Girdwood, less so as a first drive from downtown.
  • Hatcher Pass, Palmer sideAbout 1 hour 15–30 minutes (around 60 miles)Much darker than town, with big open views once you're up the valley. In winter, use the Palmer-Fishhook approach. The road over the pass toward Willow is not maintained, and the last stretch to Independence Mine often closes after snowfall. Check Alaska 511 before you go, and be ready for ice; many locals prefer a four-wheel-drive vehicle from fall through spring.

Dark skies and clouds

Look north, and put the brightest lights behind you or below you. Even a faint aurora is easier to see once your eyes have had 10 or 15 minutes to adjust, so skip the bright phone screen when you can. A headlamp with a red setting helps you walk without ruining that adjustment.

Coastal clouds are the usual spoiler here. Anchorage can be socked in while the Mat-Su, just 40 minutes north, is clear — or the other way around. If the forecast is mixed, it is often worth checking a second spot rather than standing under a solid deck. If the whole region is socked in, go home and save the gas.

A bright moon, fresh snow and wet pavement all add glare. None of that ruins a strong display, but it can hide a pale one. Give yourself more than a single night if the lights are the reason you came.

If the sky is cloudy

Don't spend a socked-in night on a cold hillside out of stubbornness. Switch to an indoor or in-town plan and try again when the forecast looks clearer.

Good backups: the weekend event list, Christmas and holiday events, and things to do in December. The Anchorage Museum's Thomas Planetarium and the University of Alaska Anchorage planetarium both run aurora-themed shows when the real sky will not cooperate. Town Square Park is fenced for a rebuild through 2026, so don't count on the old downtown rink or flower displays.

What to wear

You may be standing still for a long time, often in wind. Dress for colder than the official temperature, especially at Point Woronzof, which sits on the inlet.

  • Insulated boots and wool socks. Sneakers in a snowy lot get miserable fast.
  • A warm hat that covers your ears, and a neck gaiter or scarf.
  • Layers: a base layer, a fleece or wool mid-layer, and a windproof parka or snow jacket.
  • Gloves you can actually stand to keep on, plus a thinner pair if you are using a camera.
  • Hand and toe warmers, and a thermos. Extra camera batteries belong in an inside pocket; cold drains them.

Photo tips

A modern phone in night mode can catch a decent arc if you brace it on a railing, a beanbag or a small tripod. Turn off the flash, wait for the shot to finish, and keep headlights and bright screens pointed down so you don't wipe out someone else's view — or your own.

If you have a camera, start simple: a wide lens, manual focus set on a distant light or on infinity, and a trial exposure around 5–15 seconds at ISO 1600. If the picture is too bright, shorten the shutter or lower the ISO; if it is too dark, go the other way. A tripod is worth more than a fancy lens. Shoot a few frames, then look up — the show is better with your own eyes.

If you would rather not drive

You don't need a tour if you have a car, winter gear and a little patience. A guided run is a good fit if you don't want to drive icy roads after dark, you don't have a car, or you'd like a driver who can chase a hole in the clouds. Sightings are still never guaranteed; a careful operator will say so up front.

Book an aurora tour, or stay under a darker sky

Small-group tours leave from Anchorage with hotel pickup. If you're here for a few nights and want a darker sky out the door, consider a stay in Palmer or along the Knik, outside the brightest city lights.

Common questions

Can you see the northern lights from Anchorage?

Yes, from darker spots around the edges of town and a short drive north or into the hills. You are farther south than Fairbanks, so the display usually has to be a bit stronger, and city lights matter more. Sightings are never guaranteed, so plan for more than one night and check the live forecast before you go out.

Can you see the northern lights from downtown Anchorage?

Almost never well. Streetlights and the glow of the bowl wash out all but the strongest displays. Give yourself at least a short drive to a darker, north-facing spot such as Point Woronzof or Glen Alps.

When is the best time of year to see the northern lights near Anchorage?

The sky is dark enough from about mid-August through mid-April. September through March is the most reliable stretch. Activity often picks up around the September and March equinoxes, and December and January give you the most hours of darkness.

What time of night is best?

The lights can appear any time after dark, but the best window is often about 10 PM to 3 AM Alaska time, with many of the stronger displays clustering near midnight. A bright moon will wash out a faint aurora.

Where should I go to see the northern lights near Anchorage?

Point Woronzof is the closest (about 15 minutes from downtown). Glen Alps sits higher above the city, about 25–30 minutes up the hillside. For darker skies and a wide northern horizon, try the Eklutna Tailrace and Knik River Valley (about 45 minutes) or the Eagle River Nature Center (about 40 minutes). Hatcher Pass is darker still, with longer winter-road caveats.

What if the sky is cloudy?

Clouds end more Anchorage aurora nights than a quiet sun does. Sometimes the Mat-Su or the Knik River valley is clearer than town, and sometimes it is not. If the whole region is socked in, switch to an indoor or holiday plan — the weekend list, Christmas events, or December guide — and try again on a clearer night.

Do I need a guided aurora tour?

No, if you have a car, winter gear, and a plan for icy roads. A guided tour is a good fit if you don't want to drive after dark, you don't have a car, or you'd like a driver who can chase a hole in the clouds. Sightings are still never guaranteed.
